### D1. When a tenant says the floor is too cold, whose hands hold the step-by-step checklist?

**Our pick:** The air-con technician from the maintenance company uses the checklist. The front desk asks 3 quick questions first. The Keppel manager watches over it and does not climb ladders.

- **Exactly:** The FM contractor's technician (labelled 'FM technician (contractor or in-house)') works the on-site checklist. The helpdesk works only the top section of the same screen: 3 intake questions at the first call. Each step carries a role tag, so the helpdesk never sees technician-only steps. The 2-week BMS trend step belongs to the BMS operator. In the demo, helpdesk and BMS operator steps show their role tag and appear as done by that role (mock data), not as separate logins. The AOM supervises and does not tick boxes. The tenant gets only 2 or 3 hands-off tips, written as fixed text in the signed pill and given by the building manager or customer service lead. The AOM sees a tip only when it names a cause or a cost.
- **Why this one:** In an office tower the hands-on work is done by the FM contractor's technicians, and the deeper diagnosis by its chief engineer and BMS operator. That is where the know-how gap shows: the newcomer measures 23.5 deg C, raises the setpoint by 1 degree and closes the ticket as 'adjusted' (scenarios-detailed.md:1316, 97). So the checklist has to be in the technician's hand at that moment. The helpdesk's 3 questions often point to the cause before anyone climbs a ladder. One screen with role tags means no extra user type to build. Putting tips in the signed pill keeps routine clicks off the AOM's screen.
- **Kept from the team plan:** The checklist as the core tool. Experts 'on the ground or remote' get it (team-meeting-2026-10-02.md:31). The 24-hour idea, so a lone night technician gets the same checklist. Tenant self-serve survives as a few approved hands-off tips.

**Paths we did not take:**

- **The building helpdesk works the whole checklist.** Why not: Helpdesk staff cannot measure at desk height, open a ceiling or read a VAV box. They can triage but cannot diagnose. Better when: For a pill that is all phone triage, such as after-hours air-con bookings or deciding which complaints are urgent.

### D3. When do we count a problem as really fixed, who says so, and how long do we wait?

**Our pick:** A closed ticket is not a fix. It only counts as fixed when the senior engineer writes down why it happened, the building data looks right for two weeks, the tenant says it is better, and the floor stays quiet for 30 days.

- **Exactly:** Two outcomes plus a waiting window. 'Attended, relief given' is marked by the technician. It closes the ticket and the contractor's response clock, and changes nothing in the pill. 'Fixed' needs four things: (a) the chief engineer writes the root cause and the 'do not undo' note into the handover; (b) the BMS operator confirms the trend for the affected zones stays in band at the complaint hours for 14 days; (c) the call-back on day 7 to 14 says it is better; (d) no comfort complaint from the same floor and tenant inside 30 days. The building manager (property manager side) presses 'Fixed', so the contractor does not mark its own work, and never the tenant alone. A repeat inside 30 days is logged as evidence AGAINST that fix. The 14 days, the call-back day and the 30 days are editable fields per rule (longer for leaks). In the demo the clock is shown with dated mock tickets, with no live timer.
- **Why this one:** Every building person knows a closed ticket is not a fixed fault. In the Level 23 story five tickets close on time, and a 'closed means solved' brain becomes sure of two opposite fixes (setpoint up, then down). The same-spot rule is the veterans' own rule.
