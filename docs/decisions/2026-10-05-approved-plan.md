## The decisions

### D1. When a tenant says the floor is too cold, whose hands hold the step-by-step checklist?

**Decision:** The FM contractor's technician (labelled 'FM technician (contractor or in-house)') works the on-site checklist. The helpdesk works only the top section of the same screen: 3 intake questions at the first call. Each step carries a role tag, so the helpdesk never sees technician-only steps. The 2-week BMS trend step belongs to the BMS operator. In the demo, helpdesk and BMS operator steps show their role tag and appear as done by that role (mock data), not as separate logins. The AOM supervises and does not tick boxes. The tenant gets only 2 or 3 hands-off tips, written as fixed text in the signed pill and given by the building manager or customer service lead. The AOM sees a tip only when it names a cause or a cost.

**Why:** In an office tower the hands-on work is done by the FM contractor's technicians, and the deeper diagnosis by its chief engineer and BMS operator. That is where the know-how gap shows: the newcomer measures 23.5 deg C, raises the setpoint by 1 degree and closes the ticket as 'adjusted' (scenarios-detailed.md:1316, 97). So the checklist has to be in the technician's hand at that moment. The helpdesk's 3 questions often point to the cause before anyone climbs a ladder. One screen with role tags means no extra user type to build. Putting tips in the signed pill keeps routine clicks off the AOM's screen.

**Kept from the team plan:** The checklist as the core tool. Experts 'on the ground or remote' get it (team-meeting-2026-10-02.md:31). The 24-hour idea, so a lone night technician gets the same checklist. Tenant self-serve survives as a few approved hands-off tips.

**Not picked:**

- **The building helpdesk works the whole checklist.** Why not: Helpdesk staff cannot measure at desk height, open a ceiling or read a VAV box. They can triage but cannot diagnose. Better when: For a pill that is all phone triage, such as after-hours air-con bookings or deciding which complaints are urgent.
- **A Keppel staff member (the AOM) works the checklist.** Why not: The AOM is 'usually not the hands-on engineer' (scenarios-detailed.md:155). If the AOM ticks boxes, the demo loses the supervision story that diagram question 4 asks for. Better when: In Keppel's Singapore colocation data centres, where Keppel group companies run the site with their own staff (scenarios-detailed.md:160, 164).
- **The tenant self-serves the checklist first (team plan).** Why not: Tenants may not touch base-building air-con, ceilings or power. Making them try first can look like stalling, since the contractor is paid to turn up. Better when: Only for hands-off tips on the tenant's own side. We keep those as fixed text in the pill.

**If we are wrong:** Low. If a Keppel tower uses the property manager's own technicians, the job is the same and only the employer on the label changes. The label already says 'contractor or in-house'.

### D2. Who exactly are 'the expert' and 'the senior manager', who pays their salary, and where does Keppel's AOM fit?

**Decision:** Two ladders. The expert is the FM contractor's chief engineer, who contributes know-how, is the first step up the fix ladder, writes the cause notes and golden tests, and gives a 'technically checked' tick on every pill change. The chief engineer never signs. The team's 'senior manager' splits in two. The fix ladder runs technician, then chief engineer, then specialist or controls contractor. The approval ladder runs AOM, then asset manager, by size of spend. The building manager picks the fix with the chief engineer (TE1 step 7) and presses 'Fixed'. One signing rule everywhere: the shared core, v1.0 included, gets the chief engineer's tick plus the HQ pill owner's signature; each building's site layer is signed by that building's AOM. So v1.0 goes live with the chief engineer's tick, the HQ pill owner's signature on the core and the pilot building AOM's signature on its site layer. In buildings run by a joint-venture property manager (MBFC and One Raffles Quay, via RQAM), an update is offered through the property manager and needs its agreement. The full 13-role map is in role_map. The demo uses a 4-role switcher: technician, chief engineer, AOM, HQ pill owner (role-played and labelled).

**Why:** In a contractor-run tower, the person who knows is rarely the person who decides, and they often work for different companies (scenarios-detailed.md:20). The chief engineer holds the 'this building' know-how but leaves when the FM contract changes (scenarios-detailed.md:238), so Keppel roles must hold the signatures. Splitting the jobs answers diagram question 4, keeps the know-how owned by Keppel, and avoids a contractor signing its client's rules. A 4th role in the switcher is needed so the HQ signature can be shown live in the learning loop.

**Kept from the team plan:** The interviewed expert who later solves hard cases (now the chief engineer). The senior-manager step after a failed checklist (now the fix ladder). 'Two senior experts approve version 1' (team-meeting-2026-10-02.md:27), now a technical tick plus a Keppel signature. An approver at HQ.

**Not picked:**

- **Team plan as spoken: one senior manager who fixes on site and approves.** Why not: In a Keppel tower nobody both climbs into the ceiling and approves spending. It also leaves no place for the AOM, so diagram question 4 has no answer. Better when: A small single building where one in-house engineer also holds the budget, or an air-con service firm that both fixes and bills.
- **All Keppel: the expert and the senior manager are both Keppel staff.** Why not: Most hands-on know-how sits with contractor staff on multi-year contracts. Pretending otherwise hides the real problem: the know-how leaves when the contract changes (scenarios-detailed.md:238). Better when: Keppel colocation data centres run by Keppel group facility-manager companies (scenarios-detailed.md:160).
- **The chief engineer co-signs v1.0 (earlier draft).** Why not: A contractor's employee signing Keppel's rules mixes up who is liable, and the signer disappears when the contract ends. Better when: A building where the chief engineer is the property manager's own staff. Even then, keep the chief engineer as reviewer for one consistent rule.

**If we are wrong:** The AOM's real title, reporting line and spending limit are not public. Our only Keppel source is an INTERNSHIP listing, not an AOM job description (PROBLEM-UNDERSTANDING.md:121; scenarios-detailed.md:511). The screens use roles, not titles, so a wrong guess moves one box on the diagram and does not change the flow.

### D3. When do we count a problem as really fixed, who says so, and how long do we wait?

**Decision:** Two outcomes plus a waiting window. 'Attended, relief given' is marked by the technician. It closes the ticket and the contractor's response clock, and changes nothing in the pill. 'Fixed' needs four things: (a) the chief engineer writes the root cause and the 'do not undo' note into the handover; (b) the BMS operator confirms the trend for the affected zones stays in band at the complaint hours for 14 days; (c) the call-back on day 7 to 14 says it is better; (d) no comfort complaint from the same floor and tenant inside 30 days. The building manager (property manager side) presses 'Fixed', so the contractor does not mark its own work, and never the tenant alone. A repeat inside 30 days is logged as evidence AGAINST that fix. The 14 days, the call-back day and the 30 days are editable fields per rule (longer for leaks). In the demo the clock is shown with dated mock tickets, with no live timer.

**Why:** Every building person knows a closed ticket is not a fixed fault. In the Level 23 story five tickets close on time, and a 'closed means solved' brain becomes sure of two opposite fixes (setpoint up, then down). The same-spot rule is the veterans' own rule. Silence alone is not proof, because tenants who give up bring heaters instead of calling (scenarios-detailed.md:1320), so the trend check and call-back are added. The building manager pressing 'Fixed' stops the contractor grading its own KPI work. Keeping 'Attended, relief given' separate lets the contractor's response clock still close fast, so the contractor has no reason to fight the tool. It also gives the demo its 'aha': the AI refuses to call the 5th opposite fix solved.

**Kept from the team plan:** The solved and not-solved fork. Solved cases logged back as case studies. The senior person steps in when it is not solved (team-meeting-2026-10-02.md:33-34). Only the meaning of 'solved' and who confirms it change.

**Not picked:**

- **Ticket closed (team plan today).** Why not: A repeat fault looks like several wins, so the brain grows more sure of the quick fix that hides the cause. Better when: One-off jobs with an instant, visible result, like replacing a light tube.
- **The tenant says it is fine.** Why not: The tenant feels better right after a setpoint change while the cause is still there. Tenants cannot judge the cause. Better when: As one input (the call-back), or for pure service jobs such as cleaning.
- **The chief engineer alone confirms 'fixed' (earlier draft).** Why not: The chief engineer works for the FM contractor that did the fix, so the contractor marks its own homework. Better when: A building where the chief engineer is the property manager's own staff and did not do the fix.
- **Long-term numbers only (surveys, renewals).** Why not: Too slow and too blurry. Effects show up months or years later. Better when: Leasing or sustainability pills, where outcomes really are slow.

**If we are wrong:** The 14-day and 30-day numbers are our guesses and may be wrong for some faults. Each is one field per rule, so changing it is cheap.

### D4. Can the brain change its own advice when cases get solved, and who has to sign a change?

**Decision:** The brain never changes itself. Confirmed 'Fixed' cases go into a 'pending evidence' pile and change nothing live. A change proposal opens in two ways: (1) an expert correction, typed by the chief engineer (shown live in the demo); (2) the team's 80 percent rule as a trigger, once a fix passes 80 percent with at least 5 confirmed cases from 2 or more zones (a setting the expert can change). The trigger shows as a counter on the 'because' card, for example '3 of 5 confirmed, no proposal yet', on a mock evidence panel labelled made-up. Either way the system drafts a plain-English diff. Signing follows D2: chief engineer's 'technically checked' tick, golden tests pass, then the HQ pill owner signs a numbered core version. Core rules flagged as safety, money or tenant wording are never a silent default: each building's AOM must actively Accept them. A site layer is signed by that building's AOM. Rule matching (IF and APPLIES WHEN to a rule ID and step list) is plain code. The model only writes the 'because' wording and drafts the diff. Golden tests (5 to 8 cases written by the chief engineer) check rule IDs and step order, never the model's text, so the gate is repeatable. Code three states only: Draft, Approved, Retired, plus a 'live version' pointer. Rollback moves the pointer. The longer lifecycle stays on the diagram. Below 5 cases the screen shows 'expert rule, low evidence' instead of a percentage.

**Why:** Keppel asks for feedback to be reviewed, validated, versioned and approved before use, with rollback. Diagram question 6 tests for silent self-learning (PROBLEM-UNDERSTANDING.md:67). With 30-day windows and one floor, the 80 percent rule could never fire inside a demo, so the live change comes from the expert correction and the 80 percent rule is shown honestly as a counter. Plain-code rule matching makes the golden tests stable on stage. A correction becoming v1.1, the answer changing, then a rollback is the demo's hero moment: about 40 seconds that show Governed, Version-controlled, Accountable, Reliable and Human-supervised.

**Kept from the team plan:** The 80 percent number (now a trigger and a visible counter). Human approval before anything is shared. Repo-style commits and versions. Two senior people approve v1.0 (now a tick plus a signature). Solved cases logged back (team-meeting-2026-10-02.md:21, 27, 35, 39).

**Not picked:**

- **Team transcript: odds move by themselves and 80 percent flips the default.** Why not: Nobody approves what a case does to the ranking. The advice changes with nobody signing, and you cannot say which version a technician saw. With 10 days and no real data, any percentage would also be invented. Better when: Only for harmless ordering, such as which intake question comes first.
- **Team summary: a human approves each case one by one.** Why not: The right instinct, but approving one ticket is not approving its effect on the ranking. The default can still drift unseen. Better when: Kept as the intake check inside our pick: each case gets a quick human check before it counts as evidence.
- **No learning after launch.** Why not: It fails 'learn through governed feedback', one of the things the solution should do (handbook-full-text.txt:231-233), and throws away the team's best idea. Better when: Red-level safety scripts (fire, lift entrapment), which change only on a formal SOP review.
- **Golden tests check the model's written answer.** Why not: Model text changes run to run, so a test gate on it can fail live on Demo Day. Better when: Never as the release gate. A human reads the wording in the diff instead.

**If we are wrong:** Keppel may have no portfolio-level owner for the shared core (see D7). Then a lead AOM signs core releases with the chief engineer's tick. The mechanism does not change. The pill owner may also become a bottleneck. Fix: low-risk wording changes go in a weekly batch.

### D5. How big is one Intelligence Pill, what is written inside it, and whose name is on it?

**Decision:** One pill = one recurring problem family: 'Repeat comfort complaints (too cold or too hot) on an office floor', focus area Technical Services. The air-con brain is the shelf that holds many pills. One YAML or JSON file per version in the GitHub repo. REQUIRED BY WED 7 OCT (v0): id, name, area, version, status, scope (office towers; leaves out data halls and central plant), owner, rules (8 to 12, each with ID, IF, THEN, BECAUSE, APPLIES WHEN, green/amber/red, source quote or 'public source'), checklist steps (who may do it, colour, why it matters), never-list, escalation table (trigger, named role, how fast), golden tests (5 cases). ADDED FRI 9 OCT WITH v1.0: technical reviewer, contributors (stored as role plus an anonymised ID, for example 'Chief engineer, Singapore office towers'), data class (contains personal data: yes or no), repeat window (30 days default, editable per rule), changelog. LATER FIELDS: 'do not undo because' notes, linked evidence cases, review date, one site-layer file per building.
