## The story every decision is built around

A tenant on Level 23 complains for the fifth time in three weeks. Mornings are freezing near the lift. Afternoons are stuffy in a new glass boardroom. Each time, a technician nudges the temperature and closes the ticket. Nobody finds the cause. Our app is how the veteran's know-how stops that loop.

**D1. Who holds the checklist: the maintenance technician**

- **What we picked:** The technician from the maintenance company works the checklist. The front desk asks three quick questions first. The Keppel manager watches over it.
- **The picture:** In a restaurant, the waiter takes the complaint and the cook fixes the dish. The manager watches and decides on refunds. The customer never walks into the kitchen. Real thing: tenant reports, front desk asks, technician checks, Keppel manager supervises.
- **Why not the other ways:** The front desk cannot open a ceiling or read the air-con box. The Keppel manager is not the hands-on engineer. Tenants are not allowed to touch building air-con.
- **What we kept from the team:** The checklist stays the heart of the app. Tenants still get a few safe tips, like "close the blinds".
- **If we are wrong:** Small. Some buildings use their own technicians. The job is the same, only the employer changes.

**D2. The expert checks, Keppel signs**

- **What we picked:** The expert is the maintenance company's senior engineer, called the chief engineer, who checks every rule change but never signs it. Keppel people sign.
- **The picture:** A football club hires a coach through an agency. The coach knows the plays. The club signs the playbook. When the coach leaves, the playbook stays. Real thing: the know-how must belong to Keppel, not to the outside firm.
- **Why not the other ways:** The team's "senior manager" does two jobs: fixing on site and approving money. In a Keppel tower those are different people. Calling everyone Keppel staff hides the real problem: know-how walks out when the contract ends.
- **What we kept from the team:** The expert who helps on hard cases. Two people approving the first version. An approver at head office.
- **If we are wrong:** The Keppel manager's real job title is not public. The screens use roles, not titles, so only one box on the diagram moves.

**D3. "Fixed" means it stays fixed**

- **What we picked:** Closing a ticket is not a fix. "Fixed" needs four checks. The cause is written down. Building data looks good for two weeks. The tenant says it is better. The floor stays quiet for a month.
- **The picture:** A bucket under a leaking tap stops the mess today. Changing the washer is the fix. You only know when the floor stays dry. Real thing: the quick visit is marked "Attended, relief given". "Fixed" waits for proof.
- **Who presses "Fixed":** The building manager, not the maintenance firm. A student does not mark their own exam.
- **Why not the other ways:** "Ticket closed" makes five quick fixes look like five wins. The tenant feeling better is not proof, because the cause can still be there.
- **What we kept from the team:** The solved or not-solved fork, and the senior person stepping in.
- **If we are wrong:** The two-week and one-month windows are our guesses. Each is one setting, cheap to change.

**D4. The brain never changes itself**

- **What we picked:** Solved cases are only evidence. A change starts when the expert corrects a rule or enough cases agree. Named people sign a new version. One click undoes it.
- **The picture:** A restaurant recipe book. Cooks write notes in the margin. The printed recipe changes only after two steps. The head chef checks it. The owner signs a new edition. The old edition is kept. Real thing: the expert ticks, tests pass, head office signs version 1.1.
- **Why not the team's way:** If scores move by themselves, the advice changes with nobody signing. Nobody can say which version a technician saw. With no real data, any percentage would be made up.
- **What we kept from the team:** The 80 percent rule stays, as a counter on screen, like "3 of 5 confirmed". Human approval and saved versions stay too.
- **If we are wrong:** Keppel may have no head office owner for shared rules. Then a lead Keppel manager signs instead. Nothing else changes.

**D5. One pill is one repeat problem**

- **What we picked:** One pill covers one repeat problem. Here, an office floor that says too cold, then too hot. The air-con brain is the shelf that holds many pills.
- **The picture:** A medicine cabinet. The cabinet is the air-con brain. One pill treats one problem, like a headache. Real thing: one pill is one file, owned by a named Keppel role.
- **Why not the other ways:** A whole brain as one pill is too big to own, test or undo. Judges may think we misread the brief. One rule per pill is too small to hold the safety rules and tests.
- **What we kept from the team:** The air-con brain, the case studies, the checklists, and a saved version for every change.
- **If we are wrong:** A judge may expect one pill per whole area. We show the shelf with this pill full and two empty slots.

**D6. Shared rules plus each building's own notes**

- **What we picked:** All towers share the main rules. Each building adds a small file of its own quirks. Every complaint is tagged with building, floor and tenant.
- **The picture:** Every school uses the same textbook. Each teacher keeps sticky notes for their own class. One note says "Ali sits by the window and gets cold". Real thing: shared rules plus a building note. So the app spots "fifth complaint from Level 23".
- **Why not the other ways:** One brain per building means every building relearns the same lesson. A brain with no place tags never notices that complaints repeat. Tagging by air-con zone misses this story. The cold and hot spots sit in different zones.
- **What we kept from the team:** Both of the team's ideas: the shared service brain and the tower brain.
- **If we are wrong:** Made-up ticket history can look staged. We keep it small and label it as made up.

**D7. Head office signs, each building can say "not here"**

- **What we picked:** Head office only signs shared updates. Each building's Keppel manager can accept an update or say "not here" with a reason. Live problems stay in the building.
- **The picture:** A bubble tea chain sends every shop a new recipe. A shop manager can say "not here, our machine is different." Head office does not take each shop's orders. Real thing: hard cases climb the building's own ladder, not head office.
- **Why not the team's way:** Sending every hard case to head office is slow. Head office does not know the floor. Forcing every update on every tower breaks where a building really is different.
- **What we kept from the team:** The head office layer, human approval before sharing, and approved lessons flowing to towers.
- **Safety exception:** Urgent safety fixes get a fast lane. Safety and money rules always need an active yes.
- **If we are wrong:** Keppel may have no head office technical team. Then a lead Keppel manager owns the shared rules.

**D8. Approved steps, always in the same order**

- **What we picked:** Steps come from know-how that people have checked and approved. The order never changes. Safety first. Then context. Then the building's computer records. Then the floor. Then fixes. The AI can only pick approved steps.
- **The picture:** A pilot's pre-flight checklist. Experienced pilots wrote it. Safety comes first. Nobody invents a new step mid-flight. Real thing: any new step the AI suggests is marked "not approved".
- **Why check the computer before the floor:** The technician often arrives after the cold hour. The reading looks normal. That is exactly the newcomer's mistake.
- **Why not the team's way:** Ranking by case odds is risky. A quick fix used 50 times would outvote one expert. Nobody approved that ranking.
- **Because you have no expert yet:** We start from public sources, and every rule says "public source". The research now running fills this in.
- **What we kept from the team:** Interviews, the AI-written report, senior checking, checklists, and case counts as evidence.

**D9. Traffic lights, plus a never-list**

- **What we picked:** Every step is green, amber or red. A few things are never allowed, for anyone.
- **The picture:** Traffic lights. Green: go. Amber: wait for a named person's yes. Red: stop and call for help.
- **Green, the technician just does it:** Ask questions, measure, read records, check the thermostat, make one small logged nudge.
- **Amber, a named yes first:** A second change on the same floor within a month. Moving a thermostat. A tenant wanting a colder floor, which the Keppel manager decides. Anything about money.
- **Red, stop and call:** Smoke, a burning smell, or a heater with a warm cord. Also faults outside the pill's rules.
- **Never:** Quote a price. Admit fault in writing. Show another tenant's data. Touch the building's controls.
- **Why not the other ways:** No line at all means a junior could be told to do permit work. Approval for everything means technicians skip the tool.
- **What we kept from the team:** The human checkpoints, escalation, and the team's money question, now an amber step.
- **If we are wrong:** The one-degree limit is our guess. Each building can change it in its own file.

**D12. A full live demo, every step simple (revised 5 Oct after YY's note)**

- **Why it changed:** YY pointed to the submission rules. The Keppel brief asks for "a live demonstration" (handbook line 259). It also asks for complete source code on GitHub (line 276). Judges score "technical quality and completeness" (line 1058). So the whole loop must run live. The earlier "thin slice plus diagrams" was too small.
- **What we picked now:** Every step of the story runs in the app. Each step is kept simple, but none is faked.
- **The picture:** A full meal from starter to dessert. Small portions, but every course is real food.
- **The six live steps, in demo order:**
  1. Capture: an interview transcript goes in. The AI drafts rules, each linked to its quote. The expert corrects them. Keppel signs version 1.0.
  2. Daily use: the front desk asks three questions. The app flags "fifth complaint from Level 23". The technician works the checklist, with a "because" card on every step.
  3. Safety lines: a red stop for a heater with a burning smell. An amber card when the tenant wants a colder floor.
  4. The Keppel manager's "Needs your decision" screen: approve, send back or escalate.
  5. Learning with undo: "Attended" versus "Fixed". The expert corrects a rule. Version 1.1 is tested, signed, used, then undone.
  6. Reuse: a second building is offered version 1.1. Its manager accepts or says "not here". Head office sees which building runs which version.
- **Still only on the roadmap:** Body cameras, real building system links, real logins, data centres. These cannot be real in 10 days.
- **How a solo builder gets there:** First make all six steps work roughly, by Fri 9 Oct. Then make each one better. Freeze, then record a backup video.
- **What we kept from the team:** All three phases. Phase 1 (capture) and Phase 2 (daily use) are now both live. Phase 3 stays on the roadmap.
