# The Level 23 story: too cold at 9am, too hot at 3pm

> Made up, Keppel-style, written for people new to buildings and checked by an operations reviewer. Not a real Keppel incident. Facts about Keppel that are not public are marked "likely".

- **Building:** Office tower
- **Trigger:** Made-up example in any Singapore office tower. The office manager of an insurance company that rents all of level 23 in a 40-storey office tower sends the fifth comfort complaint in three weeks. In the mornings, staff in the open area near the lift core (the middle of the floor, away from the windows) say it is freezing. In the afternoons, the new glass boardroom on the west side is stuffy and warm when it is full. The tenant moved in eight months ago after a fit-out (interior renovation) that split the open floor into glass meeting rooms.
- **People:** Tenant staff: the employees who actually feel too cold or too hot.; Tenant office manager: the tenant's one contact person who passes complaints to the building, and who gets blamed by their own boss if nothing changes.; Helpdesk or customer service officer: the building's front desk that logs complaints and assigns jobs. Depending on the building, employed by the property manager or by an outsourced FM company.; Building manager (from the property manager): the person who runs the building day to day for the owner.; FM contractor technician: a hands-on air-con technician from the outsourced facilities management (FM) company, or the property manager's own in-house technician.; BMS operator or technical executive: the person who watches the building management system (BMS), the computer that runs and records the air-con.; Landlord's nominated BMS contractor: the specialist company the landlord chooses to test and set up the air-con control boxes after a tenant's renovation.; Asset Operations Manager (AOM): the owner-side manager who oversees the property manager and FM contractors, and shares the tenant relationship with leasing.; Leasing manager: the person who signs and renews leases (rental contracts).; Tenant's fit-out designer and professional engineer (PE): the people who designed the tenant's interior works (fit-out) and were meant to adjust the air-con ducts for the new rooms. A PE is a licensed engineer who signs off designs.
- **Systems:** Building app, hotline or email: where tenants report faults. The maker of the Keppel Bay Tower app says it includes fault reporting and digital circulars (notices).; Work order system (CMMS, computerised maintenance management system): logs each job, who was sent, and when it was closed.; Building management system (BMS): shows temperature and airflow per zone and keeps a history (trend) of readings.; VAV box (variable air volume box): a box in the ceiling that sends more or less cold air to one zone, guided by one temperature sensor (thermostat). The target temperature it aims for is called the setpoint.; Fit-out file: the tenant's approved drawings, showing air outlets (diffusers), return air grilles (the vents that pull room air back to the air-con), VAV boxes and thermostats, plus the air balancing report (a record of how much air each outlet actually delivers after the works).; Tenant satisfaction survey results: Keppel REIT reports running regular tenant satisfaction surveys through external parties and its buildings' property managers.; Handover notes or shift log: free-text notes between shifts, often a notebook, an email thread or a WhatsApp group.

- **What goes wrong:** The tickets ping-pong. One technician fixes 'too cold' by raising the setpoint, which makes the afternoon boardroom worse, so the next ticket says 'too hot' and the next technician lowers it again. Every ticket is closed on time, so the response-time numbers look good, but the cause is never fixed: one thermostat serving two very different spaces after the fit-out, a sealed boardroom with no return air path, and a core zone that cannot turn its air down far enough. Staff bring heaters and fans. The office manager stops trusting the building and goes to their own boss, who calls the owner. This pattern can happen in any building, including well-run ones, because each single ticket was handled by the book.
- **What it costs:** Many wasted technician visits. Extra cooling energy whenever the answer is 'make it colder' (Singapore's Go 25 campaign cites a Ngee Ann Polytechnic study showing up to 12 percent energy saving for each degree raised from 23 to 25 deg C in offices). Personal heaters add energy use and an electrical risk. A lower tenant satisfaction score, and a quiet risk at lease renewal. One ticket costs little. The pattern costs the relationship.
- **Written down vs not:** Written down: fit-out rules (engineer to re-route ducts, thermostat and grille positions on plans, VAV testing by the landlord's nominated BMS contractor, return air grilles never blocked), complaint response-time targets in FM contracts, the voluntary national Go 25 guidance, green lease clauses on energy use, and the work order history. Only in heads: where each thermostat really sits after the fit-out, this floor's morning cold and sunny-afternoon patterns, which tenant contacts complain early and which only complain when it is serious, why each past setting change was made, and who should pay for which fix.
- **How it differs by building type:** Office tower: comfort is about people and feelings, many floors and zones, glass walls that heat up in the afternoon sun, after-hours air-con extensions, and a national push to run at 25 deg C or warmer. Data centre: 'comfort' mainly means the air temperature and humidity going into the servers, measured at the front of the rack and written into the service level agreement (SLA, the contract's promises). Customers often watch their own sensors, so a 'too hot' report is a contract matter backed by data, not a feeling. Keppel DC REIT says it reviews temperature setpoints in SLAs at each renewal to move closer to IMDA's (the Infocomm Media Development Authority, Singapore's digital regulator) recommendation of warmer data centres. Human comfort only applies to small customer rooms and offices. Logistics facility: many warehouses are not fully air-conditioned (general practice), so complaints are about heat for workers, ventilation and loading bay conditions; cold-storage tenants run their own refrigerated rooms and care most about power reliability. Keppel now lists logistics as non-core, so this matters less for Keppel today.
- **Crosses into:** Technical Services: re-balancing airflow, moving the thermostat, adding a VAV box, or checking whether the west-side air handler has enough capacity.; Energy Optimisation: the morning start-up time, minimum airflow and setpoints; 'make it colder' works against the 25 deg C push.; Leasing: the fit-out approval file decides who pays, and repeat complaints before a renewal are a leasing risk.; Sustainability: green lease clauses on energy use limit how cold the building should run.; Asset Operations: the AOM decides who pays and when to warn leasing.

General practice:
- Office air-con often starts before office hours to cool the floor, and core zones with no sun and few people can overcool, especially when a VAV box cannot turn its air down below its minimum airflow setting.
- A VAV damper sitting fully open for hours means the zone wants more air than it can get; changing the setpoint does not fix that.
- After a fit-out, a thermostat can end up in a different room from the people it is meant to serve, and glass rooms without a return air path trap heat, which causes 'cold here, hot there' complaints on one floor.
- Comfort tickets are often closed by a setpoint change without finding a root cause, so the same floor generates repeat tickets.
- Personal heaters and desk fans are a common sign that occupants have given up on the building's air-con, and heaters are an electrical risk.
- The reason a past setpoint or schedule change was made is often kept only in handover notes or people's memory.
- Helpdesks may be run by the property manager or by an outsourced FM company, depending on the building.
- Many warehouses are not fully air-conditioned; worker heat and ventilation are the typical comfort issues there.
