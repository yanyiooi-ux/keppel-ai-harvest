# Problem statement

> Made up, Keppel-style, for the hackathon demo. Not a real Keppel incident. All buildings, tenants, people and numbers are labelled so.

## Who has the problem

The people who feel the problem are the tenant staff on an office floor. They are too cold in the morning near the lift core, and too hot in the afternoon in the west glass boardroom. Their office manager passes the complaints to the building.

The people who must fix it are the FM contractor's technician and chief engineer. They work for an outside company on a multi-year contract. The building manager (from the property manager) picks the fix and tells the tenant what is happening. The AOM (a Keppel role) watches the cost and the tenant relationship.

## What goes wrong on Level 23 today

Level 23 of Tower A is the demo floor. The tenant, Northwind Advisory (made up), moved in 8 months ago after a fit-out that split the open floor into glass meeting rooms. Over 21 days, five comfort tickets come in:

- Three cold-morning complaints from the open area near the lift core.
- Two hot-afternoon complaints from the west glass boardroom.

Each ticket is handled by the book. The technician measures 23.5 deg C at the desk, raises the zone setpoint by 1 deg C, and closes the ticket as "adjusted". That makes the afternoon boardroom hotter, so the next ticket says "too hot" and the next technician lowers it again. The fixes ping-pong: temp up, then down, then up.

The real cause is never touched: the fit-out left one thermostat serving two very different spaces (the open area and the glass boardroom), and the glass boardroom has no return air path so it traps heat. The core zone's VAV box cannot turn its air down below its minimum airflow setting, so it keeps getting cold air in the morning no matter what the setpoint says. Staff bring personal heaters and desk fans, a sign they have stopped trusting the building.

Every ticket closes on time, so the response-time numbers look good. But the cause is never fixed. The pattern costs the relationship.

## What it costs

- Five wasted technician visits in three weeks (made-up story, D10 metric).
- Extra cooling energy whenever the answer is "make it colder". Singapore's Go 25 campaign cites up to 12 percent air-con energy saving for each degree raised from 23 to 25 deg C in offices. So every "make it colder" answer has a cost.
- Personal heaters add energy use and an electrical risk.
- A lower tenant satisfaction score, and a quiet risk at lease renewal. Northwind's lease ends in 10 months (made-up data).
- One ticket costs little. The pattern costs the relationship.

## Why the know-how walks out the door when the FM contract changes

The "this building" know-how sits with the FM contractor's chief engineer and senior technicians. They hold: where each thermostat really sits after the fit-out, this floor's morning-cold and sunny-afternoon patterns, which tenant contacts complain early and which only complain when it is serious, why each past setting change was made, and who should pay for which fix.

Their contract runs 3 to 6 years. When it ends, the chief engineer and technicians leave, and they take the building's quirks with them. BCA's guide asks for a mobilisation period so the new contractor can take over, which shows the gap is known, but the know-how in people's heads is not written down in a form the next person can find.

The work order system records WHAT happened (job closed, response time met) far better than WHY it happened or what was learned. Close-out notes say "rectified" or "adjusted", which teaches nothing. The handover notes or shift log that might hold the real reason are often a notebook, an email thread or a chat group.
