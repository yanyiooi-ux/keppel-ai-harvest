# Who is who in a Keppel-style building

> Made up, Keppel-style, written for people new to buildings and checked by an operations reviewer. Not a real Keppel incident. Facts about Keppel that are not public are marked "likely".

A big Keppel-style portfolio is run in layers, like a company inside a company. At the top, investors own units in a REIT (a trust listed on the stock exchange that owns buildings and pays out the rent). A trustee holds the buildings legally for them. The REIT manager (Keppel REIT Management for offices, Keppel DC REIT Management for data centres) sets strategy, rents, budgets and big repairs. Below that, a property manager runs each building day to day. For Ocean Financial Centre and Keppel Bay Tower that is a Keppel company. For Marina Bay Financial Centre and One Raffles Quay it is RQAM, a manager Keppel shares with two partner developers, because Keppel only part-owns those towers. Keppel's asset operations staff manage the outsourced FM (facilities management) companies and review SOPs (standard operating procedures, the written "how we do it" steps). The hands-on work (daily rounds, repairs, maintenance) is mostly done by the FM contractor's chief engineer and technicians, plus specialist subcontractors for chillers, lifts, fire systems and cooling-tower water. Independent professionals and government agencies (BCA, SCDF, NEA, EMA, MOM) set a fixed calendar of yearly, 3-yearly and 5-yearly checks.

The key pattern: decisions and money sit high up (REIT manager, Asset Operations Manager). The "this building" know-how sits low down (chief engineer, senior technician, specialist), often in a different company whose contract ends every 3 to 6 years. The systems record WHAT happened (job tickets, alarms, KPI scores, which are the numbers a contractor is paid against) far better than WHY it happened or what was learned.

Offices and data centres differ. Offices run on office-hours rhythms: tenant comfort, after-hours air-con, fit-outs (tenants building their office inside the space) and leases of about 3 years. Most faults are comfort or nuisance problems, judged by how fast they are answered. Keppel's Singapore colocation data centres (Keppel runs the building and clients rent space and power for their servers) are run 24/7 by Keppel group facility manager companies. They use shift teams, strict written scripts (SOP, MOP, EOP), change control, and tight promises on uptime and temperature, so one mistake can cause an outage. In triple-net data centres (the client rents the whole building and pays most running costs) the client often runs the facility itself, so Keppel sees less of the daily work. Logistics is non-core for Keppel today. The end-to-end flow below is a MADE-UP office example. The leak points apply to both building types.

### Systems

| System | Plain meaning | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|
| BMS (building management system) | The central computer that shows and controls the chillers, air handlers, pumps, fans and lights, and raises alarms when a number crosses a set line. | FM technicians, BMS operators and the chief engineer. Energy and analytics teams read its data. Keppel Bay Tower's digital twin runs on live BMS data. |
| CMMS or work order system (computerised maintenance management system) | A job-ticket system. Every fault gets a ticket with a priority, the person assigned and the time stamps. It also holds the asset list and the preventive maintenance schedule, and produces the KPI numbers. | Helpdesk, technicians, the chief engineer, the property manager, and the AOM (through reports) |
| Fault reporting channels (hotline, email, tenant app or portal) | How tenants tell the building something is wrong. Keppel Bay Tower has a tenant mobile app (described for visitor e-invitations). Fault reporting through apps or hotlines is common practice. | Tenants' office managers and the helpdesk |
| Fault detection and diagnostics, and digital twin | Rules and machine learning that spot equipment running badly. At Keppel Bay Tower this gives daily advice on settings and flags faults. | Building operators, energy engineers, Keppel technology teams |
| Keppel Operations Nerve Centre (ONC) | Keppel's remote control room for watching and tuning cooling and energy systems 24/7 with data analytics and AI. | Keppel's infrastructure and cooling teams. Whether it watches Keppel REIT office towers is not confirmed. |
| DCIM and power monitoring (data centres) | DCIM (data centre infrastructure management) shows power, temperature and capacity for every room and rack, so staff can see a hot spot or an overloaded circuit. | Data centre shift engineers and site leads (general practice; Keppel's tool names are not public) |
| SOP, MOP, EOP and WI document library | Written procedures: normal steps (SOP), step-by-step scripts for risky changes (MOP), emergency steps (EOP) and task instructions (WI). | Data centre and FM engineers. They write and use them. The AOM reviews building SOPs. |
| Shift logbook and handover sheet | A record of what happened on each shift: live alarms, equipment out of service, work in progress, and anything odd the next shift must watch. | 24/7 shift teams in data centres and large towers (general practice) |
| Permit-to-work system | A form, on paper or digital, that must be approved before risky work such as work at height, hot work (welding) or entering a confined space. It names the hazards and the safety steps. | FM contractor supervisors, subcontractors, and the property manager or safety officer who signs |
| Monthly FM report and KPI scorecard | Usually a spreadsheet or slide pack: jobs opened and closed, response times against the SLA (service level agreement, the promised response times), preventive maintenance done, energy use, incidents, and the status of statutory inspections. | Written by the FM contractor. Read by the property manager and AOM. It decides bonus or deduction payments. |
| Lease management and property accounting system | Holds each lease: rent, end date, renewal options, service charges and after-hours air-con billing. | Leasing, finance and the property manager (general practice; Keppel's system is not public) |
| Government portals | BCA's BESS (yearly energy data), BCA's LEAP (lift and escalator Permits to Operate), GoBusiness (SCDF contact details), and EMA's licensing portal (electrical licences). | The property manager, the appointed professionals and the LEW |
| Access control and security systems | Gates, cards, face recognition and CCTV. Keppel Bay Tower uses face recognition for building access. | Security contractor, property manager, tenants |
| Email, phone and chat groups | Where much real coordination and decision-making happens: quotes, approvals, 'why we did it' explanations, and photos sent by technicians. | Everyone (general practice; not verified for Keppel) |

### Rhythms

- **Every shift (24/7 sites: data centres, and big towers with night shifts):** Shift handover: live alarms, equipment out of service, open jobs and temporary set-ups are passed on, partly in writing and partly by talking. (General practice.)
- **Daily:** Plant-room rounds and meter readings. Air-con start-up before office hours. A review of BMS alarms and the open job list. Toolbox talk (a short safety briefing) before risky work. Data centre environment checks. At Keppel Bay Tower the fault-detection system gives daily setting advice.
- **Weekly:** An operations meeting between the property manager and the FM contractor on open jobs, upcoming shutdowns, contractor works and tenant issues. (General practice.)
- **Monthly:** The FM contractor sends the monthly report. KPI scores are checked against a performance target, which in BCA's example rises from 80% in year 1 to 88% from year 4, and pay can move up or down by at most 10% of the monthly fee. A monthly management meeting follows. NEA requires a cooling-tower water bacteria count (standard plate count) at least once a month. Many preventive maintenance tasks run on monthly or quarterly cycles.
- **Quarterly:** NEA requires a Legionella test of cooling-tower water at least once every three months. Keppel publishes quarterly business updates, and REIT managers report occupancy and leasing to investors.
- **Half-yearly:** REIT half-year results. Fire drills and table-top exercises run by the Fire Safety Manager (frequency seen only on consultant websites).
- **Yearly:** A PE inspects fire systems and the FC (Fire Certificate) form goes to SCDF. Lift and escalator tests are witnessed by an Authorised Examiner, and the Permit to Operate is renewed on BCA's LEAP portal. The EMA electrical installation licence is renewed through the LEW. Energy data goes to BCA. The FM contractor gets a yearly performance appraisal (BCA guide). Tenant satisfaction surveys (Keppel REIT says 'regular'). Budget and capex planning, and sustainability reporting (Green Mark, GRESB).
- **Every 3 years:** From 1 April 2026, buildings with a good record renew the Fire Certificate once every 3 years, but still need the yearly PE inspection. When BCA sends a notice, the chiller plant gets a periodic energy audit, reportedly about every 3 years. A typical FM contract runs 3 years plus an option of 3 more.
- **Every 5 years and longer:** Periodic Structural Inspection by a Structural Engineer for non-residential buildings every 5 years. Facade inspection every 7 years for buildings over 13 m tall and over 20 years old (seen in search results only). Energy-intensive buildings can be placed under BCA's Mandatory Energy Improvement regime: an audit, then a 10% cut in energy use within 3 years, held for 1 year.
- **Lease cycle (offices):** Singapore office leases usually run about 3 years. Fit-out at the start, then rent reviews and renewal talks well before the end, then reinstatement works before the tenant leaves. Keppel REIT in 9M 2025: 96.3% committed occupancy, WALE (weighted average lease expiry, the average time left on leases weighted by rent) of 4.7 years portfolio-wide, +12.0% rent change on renewals, and 74.9% tenant retention.
- **Contract cycle (data centres):** Keppel DC REIT starts renewal talks with clients at least six months before contracts end, and runs a 'Know Your Customer' check before signing or renewing. Colocation contracts gave 73.9% of rental income in 2024.

### Rules and regulators

- BCA, Building Control Act Part IIIB (existing buildings): owners must meet minimum green standards when making a major energy-use change, submit energy use and building data every year, and carry out a periodic energy audit of the cooling system when BCA sends a notice, using a PE (Mechanical) or a BCA-registered Energy Auditor.
- BCA, Mandatory Energy Improvement regime (gazetted 11 Oct 2024): owners of energy-intensive buildings must audit, submit an improvement plan, cut energy use intensity by 10% within 3 years, and hold that for 1 year.
- BCA, Periodic Structural Inspection: non-residential buildings once every 5 years. The owner appoints a Structural Engineer and fixes what is found. In strata buildings the MCST counts as the owner.
- BCA, lifts and escalators: a valid Permit to Operate is needed, applied for and renewed through the LEAP portal, with yearly testing witnessed by an independent Authorised Examiner.
- BCA, Building Maintenance and Strata Management Act: in strata buildings the MCST must maintain common property and may appoint a managing agent.
- BCA guides (advisory, not law): Guide on FM Procurement (outcome-based contracts, monthly KPI scoring, 3+3-year terms, a mobilisation period for taking over from the previous contractor) and Guide to Smart FM.
- SCDF, Fire Safety Act: every building needs a valid Fire Certificate. From 1 April 2026 buildings with a good record get a 3-year certificate, but a PE must still inspect fire systems every year and the FC form must be sent each year. A late form means the certificate is revoked, plus fines or prosecution. SCDF audits at random. Certain large premises must appoint a Fire Safety Manager.
- NEA, Environmental Public Health (Cooling Towers and Water Fountains) Regulations and Code of Practice: standard plate count at least monthly, Legionella count at least every three months, plus record keeping and cleaning rules.
- EMA, Electricity Act licensing: non-domestic installations above 45 kVA need an electrical installation licence, renewed each year through an appointed Licensed Electrical Worker.
- MOM, Workplace Safety and Health Act and regulations: risk assessment and permit-to-work for high-risk jobs (confined spaces by law; work at height, hot work and live electrical work as standard practice). Keppel REIT's property managers hold bizSAFE Star certification.
- IMDA and EDB (data centres): the second Data Centre Call for Application requires Green Mark for Data Centres Platinum and a PUE (power usage effectiveness, total power divided by IT power) of 1.25 or better at full IT load.
- Keppel internal: Keppel's '10 Commandments for Safe AI' require approved tools and human oversight (from our earlier sourced file).

### Where know-how leaks today

- At the very first phone call: the tenant's 'this happened before' and other context is spoken to the helpdesk but not typed into the ticket.
- At ticket close-out: notes like 'rectified' or 'cleared choke' record that the job was done, not why it happened or how it was really fixed, so the next technician starts from zero.
- At shift handover in 24/7 sites: the spoken part ('watch pump 2, it sounded rough') is lost if it is not written down. Uptime Institute says failure to follow procedures, or bad procedures, is behind most human-error outages.
- When the FM contract changes, often after 3 or 6 years: the outgoing chief engineer and technicians take the building's quirks with them. BCA's guide asks for a mobilisation period just so the new contractor can take over from the old one and set up documentation, which shows the gap is known.
- When technicians are rotated between sites, resign or retire: BCA says about 40% of the built environment's professional and technical workforce in Singapore is 50 or older.
- Between companies: the know-how sits with the FM contractor and subcontractors, while decisions and accountability sit with Keppel. The person who knows is rarely the person who decides, and they work for different firms.
- In specialist reports: chiller service reports, PE fire inspection findings, Authorised Examiner lift reports and water treatment logs arrive as PDFs or paper. They are filed, but not linked to the asset history or followed up in one place.
- In the monthly FM report: it measures speed (percentage of jobs within the SLA), so a repeat fault fixed fast twice looks green both times. Patterns across months are seen only by someone who reads and remembers.
- Between operations and leasing: tenant pain (repeat leaks, heat complaints, after-hours air-con bills) sits in the helpdesk system, while renewal decisions sit in the leasing file. Only a veteran links them before renewal talks.
- Between fit-out and operations: changes made during tenant fit-outs (re-routed pipes, extra loads) are approved on paper by the property manager but often do not make it into the as-built drawings or the CMMS asset list.
- Across buildings: the same lesson is relearned at each building, because different towers have different property managers (a Keppel company versus the RQAM joint venture), different FM contractors and different report formats.
- In co-owned buildings: for Marina Bay Financial Centre and One Raffles Quay, the operating know-how sits in RQAM, a manager Keppel shares with partners, so Keppel may not own or easily reuse it.
- In data centres on triple-net leases: the client runs the facility, so Keppel never sees the daily operating know-how for those assets.
- When an asset is sold or bought: Keppel is selling non-core assets, including logistics and hospitality. A building's history leaves with the sale, and an acquired building arrives with only data-room documents, not the people who know it.
- In 'why' decisions: why a setting was changed, why we repaired instead of replaced, or why a tenant got goodwill is decided by email, chat or phone and is not captured where the next person will look.
- In AI and analytics tools: the ONC, digital twins and fault detection see the data but not the site context (for example 'that sensor always reads high'). That context stays with site staff unless someone writes it down.
